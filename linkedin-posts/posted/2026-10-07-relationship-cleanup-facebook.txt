A feature on my platform was quietly inventing companies. Here's how I found it and fixed it.

My AI Healthcare Intelligence platform has a Company Relationship Graph — it reads healthcare AI news and maps out who's partnering with whom, who's acquiring whom, who's investing in whom. Useful, when it works.

While reviewing it, I noticed some of the "companies" in the data weren't companies at all. "2025." "Agreement." "Re." Nonsense strings sitting in a feature meant to track real business relationships.

Here's why: the original extraction logic was a keyword-window heuristic. It found a trigger phrase like "acquires" or "partners with" in an article, then grabbed a few words on either side and called that the company name. No actual entity recognition — just proximity to a keyword. It worked often enough to look reliable, which is exactly what made the garbage entries easy to miss at first glance.

I had two options. Write a denylist of bad words and chase every new flavor of garbage forever. Or fix the real problem.

I rebuilt the extraction using the same pattern already powering deal-signal detection elsewhere in the app: instead of a word-window guess, Claude reads the keyword-matched candidate articles directly and extracts the real company names. Then I added a second layer — every extracted name gets checked against the 627-company directory the platform already maintains, so name variants of the same company ("R1" vs "R1 RCM") collapse into one canonical entity instead of persisting as two different "companies."

Then I ran a retroactive cleanup pass against the live production data to fix what was already there, not just prevent new garbage going forward.

The result: 116 relationships in the table before cleanup. 58 after. Roughly half the table was duplicate noise — and in the process, I found a second, unrelated bug: the app's own duplicate check was case-sensitive, so near-identical articles producing slightly different capitalization of the same extraction had been slipping past it for weeks, accumulating silently.

None of this is glamorous. It's the unglamorous part of building real AI features: the data has to actually be true, not just look impressive in a demo. A graph full of fake companies is worse than no graph at all, because it looks authoritative while being wrong.

Three healthcare AI companies raised $1.35 billion in a single day yesterday.

Angle Health: $600 million at a $2.7 billion valuation.
Ortet: $500 million committed to a new health-focused AI lab from the ground up.
OpenEvidence: $250 million — at a reported $15 billion valuation.

That last number is the one worth sitting with.

OpenEvidence is a clinical AI tool that helps physicians find and synthesize medical evidence faster. It's genuinely useful — physicians who use it tend to like it. But $15 billion puts it at roughly 30% of Epic's estimated value, and Epic handles patient records for 38% of every hospital in America. OpenEvidence has not achieved anything close to that level of market penetration.

What investors are really pricing is a thesis: that AI becomes the default layer between clinicians and medical knowledge, that most clinical decision-making runs through AI interfaces within 10 years, and that whoever builds the trusted infrastructure for that wins a market worth hundreds of billions.

That's a coherent bet. It may even be right. But it's a bet on a future that hasn't happened yet, priced as if it's already inevitable.

Three signals worth watching from today's data:

First, the money is moving fast. $1.35B in one day is not normal. When capital concentrates this quickly around a category, it usually means the window for second-movers is closing.

Second, the "AI lab" model is back. Ortet's $500M commitment to a standalone health AI lab — not an acquisition, not a product extension, but a new research organization — suggests some investors believe the foundational AI work in healthcare hasn't been done yet and needs dedicated infrastructure.

Third, valuations are decoupling from current revenue. A $15B valuation on a company that is still in early market penetration is a signal that the market is pricing outcomes, not run rates. That dynamic historically ends one of two ways.

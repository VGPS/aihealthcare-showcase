Here's a concentration-of-power story in healthcare AI that almost nobody is telling:

38% of U.S. hospital patient records — roughly 305 million patients — flow through Epic Systems. And Epic runs an app marketplace called App Orchard, where healthcare AI vendors apply to be listed, go through a security and integration review, and get approved or not. That process takes months, carries costs, and operates entirely under rules that Epic sets and can change.

Epic also builds AI products of its own. Sepsis prediction. Deterioration scoring. Readmission risk. All embedded by default in the workflow that 38% of U.S. hospital clinicians use every day.

For healthcare AI companies, this creates a distribution dynamic that is rarely named directly in the industry:

Being "Epic-integrated" has become table stakes for enterprise hospital sales. Most health system procurement teams won't seriously evaluate a clinical AI tool that requires clinicians to work in a separate application outside the EHR. The pitch is "embedded in your existing workflow" or it's an uphill battle.

That means Epic sits at the top of the healthcare AI distribution stack — approving or delaying access to the most valuable channel in the category, competing with vendors in that channel, and setting rules that apply to everyone except itself.

The Apple App Store drew antitrust scrutiny over a 30% revenue cut and preferential first-party placement. Epic's structure is different — it's not a revenue cut, it's a gatekeeping and workflow-embedding mechanism. But the competitive dynamics are comparable, and the regulatory attention hasn't caught up.

If you're building healthcare AI, your Epic strategy isn't a business development question. It's a risk assessment.

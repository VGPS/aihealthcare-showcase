NEWSLETTER — "Four Outages, One Week, and the Staffing Chart Nobody's Showing You"
==================================================

On November 16, 2026, a software bug in an on-premise hardware system at JPMorgan Chase delayed transactions at six major U.S. banks. The same day, United Airlines paused departures for 90 minutes after a system outage knocked out pilot-facing weight and balance tools, delaying more than 700 flights. Three days earlier, a malfunctioning router overwhelmed the FAA's air traffic control system and grounded every flight in the country for nearly five hours. The Thursday before that, a Microsoft outage took down Outlook and several AI products for close to 48 hours, affecting roughly 140,000 people across 74 countries.

Four companies. Four different official causes. None of them name AI.

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin raised the question publicly anyway, floating an AI theory for the cluster of failures, while noting a cyberattack looked unlikely as the common thread.

The official causes and the layoff numbers

None of this week's outages has a confirmed AI-related root cause. Chase called its issue a hardware bug, not cyber-related. United and the FAA pointed to a system outage and an aging air traffic control network running on a malfunctioning new router. Microsoft has not published a root cause as of this writing.

What is confirmed, separately, is the scale of the layoffs reshaping the teams that keep these systems running. U.S. tech companies have cut more than 206,000 jobs in 2026 across 715 separate events, according to the layoff tracker TrueUp — above 2025's total of roughly 211,000 and a reversal of what had looked like a cooling trend. Employer disclosures tracked by Challenger, Gray & Christmas show 121,401 of this year's cuts explicitly attributed to AI, more than double all of 2025. AI's share of announced layoffs rose from about 5% in 2025 to roughly 23% in the first half of 2026.

Where the cuts are landing

The layoffs are not landing evenly. A Harvard study covering 62 million workers found that junior developer employment drops 9 to 10% within six quarters of a company adopting generative AI tools. Forbes reports junior software developer employment has declined for 33 consecutive months. Stanford's 2026 AI Index found employment among 22- to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed jobs sat 19% below trend by June 2026, with no comparable gap for experienced workers.

Axonius, a cybersecurity company, cut 66 jobs this year, including 22% of its Israeli developers, as its CEO described reshaping engineering around a new AI-driven way of working. It is one concrete example of a pattern playing out across the industry: fewer junior hires, AI tools covering the gap, senior engineers absorbing more systems with less backup.

The counter-argument

Not everyone agrees AI is the primary driver. Cognizant's Chief AI Officer, cited in Nikkei Asia's reporting, has argued AI is being used as cover for ordinary restructuring and a correction after pandemic-era over-hiring. Layoffs.fyi has identified a correlation between AI adoption and headcount reductions in 2026 but describes the relationship as neither uniform nor conclusively causal.

Both things can be true. Some of this year's cuts are AI-labeled restructuring that would have happened anyway. Some of it is a real substitution of junior engineering labor for AI tooling. The employer surveys suggest the AI-attributed share is real and growing, not just a label of convenience: it more than doubled year over year.

The question this week's outages actually raise

None of this proves AI caused Monday's bank outage or last week's grounded flights. The official explanations point to hardware, networking, and legacy infrastructure, the same categories that have caused outages for decades.

What's different now is the staffing underneath those systems. The junior engineer who used to learn a payment system or a flight-dispatch tool by maintaining it alongside a senior engineer is less likely to have been hired in the first place. The senior engineers who remain are covering more ground, with fewer people who have handled this exact failure mode before.

When a Microsoft 365 outage knocks out email, it also knocks out the AI tools some of those thinned-out teams now lean on to triage incidents, an outage inside the outage. Losing institutional knowledge is not the same as losing code. It shows up later, in how long a root cause takes to find and how many systems break the same way twice.

Four outages in one week do not settle the AI-layoffs debate. They do raise a specific, answerable question for any company watching from outside: if something like this happened here, how many people on staff would actually recognize the failure, and how many would be reading the documentation for the first time.

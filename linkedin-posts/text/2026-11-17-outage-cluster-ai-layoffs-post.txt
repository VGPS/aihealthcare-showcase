This week: Microsoft's email system went down for close to 48 hours. The FAA grounded every flight in the country for nearly five hours. United's departure system failed mid-dispatch, stranding pilots without weight and balance numbers. Chase, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, US Bank, Truist, and Capital One all had a multi-hour outage that delayed Zelle payments nationwide.

Four unrelated companies. Four different official explanations — a hardware bug, a malfunctioning router, an aging air traffic control system, and, for Microsoft, no public root cause yet. None of them name AI.

A Homeland Security official asked the AI question publicly anyway.

Here's what's true independent of that answer: U.S. tech companies have laid off more than 206,000 workers in 2026. Employers now cite AI in roughly 1 in 4 of those layoffs, versus 1 in 20 last year. Junior developer hiring has fallen for 33 straight months. A Harvard study of 62 million workers found junior developer headcount drops 9 to 10% within six quarters of a company adopting generative AI.

The entry-level engineer who used to learn a system by maintaining it next to a senior engineer isn't getting hired as often anymore. The senior engineers who remain are covering more systems, with fewer people who've touched them before.

That doesn't prove AI caused any of this week's outages. It means every one of them happened inside organizations carrying less human redundancy than they had two years ago.

I don't know what broke Monday. I don't think the companies fully know yet either. I'd watch how many of the post-mortems mention a recent reorg before they mention a root cause.

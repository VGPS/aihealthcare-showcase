Three different groups studied the same question this fall, with no coordination between them. All three landed on the exact same answer.

Pew Research Center and WBFF polling (reaffirmed again in August, "no significant shift"), a Bloomberg analysis of coverage-decision attitudes, and a separate skepticism survey all converged within one week of each other on this: Americans are fine with AI in healthcare — right up until it stops assisting and starts deciding.

The pattern holds across all three: people support AI as a diagnostic tool or decision-support layer, as long as a physician keeps final authority. They want to be told when AI touched their care. And they reject, specifically, AI making autonomous calls on two things — medical diagnoses and insurance coverage. Not "AI in healthcare, period." Just those two decision points, consistently, across every poll that asked.

That's a much more precise signal than the usual "public distrusts AI" headline. It's not blanket rejection. It's a boundary — assist, don't decide — that's held steady since at least August, through three independently-run studies, with zero convergence toward acceptance of autonomous AI medical or coverage decisions.

For anyone building or deploying healthcare AI, that boundary isn't an obstacle to design around. It's the actual shape of the market you're building for.

---
Ask Americans if they're okay with AI in healthcare, and most will say yes. Ask them if they're okay with an algorithm deciding whether their insurance claim gets covered, and the answer flips completely.

A Bloomberg analysis of public opinion research, published this month, found Americans overwhelmingly reject AI making autonomous decisions about medical treatment or insurance coverage — even while they're fine with AI helping gather clinical information or assist a diagnosis. Separate polling from Pew Research and WBFF, running since at least this spring and reaffirmed again in August with "no significant shift," found the identical pattern: conditional support for AI as a tool, hard rejection of AI as the final word on coverage.

That distinction — AI assisting a human decision vs. AI making the decision — is exactly the line several states have started writing into law. Georgia's SB 444, for one, prohibits insurance coverage decisions made "based solely on artificial intelligence." The public sentiment and the emerging legislation aren't coincidentally aligned; the legislation is a direct response to the sentiment.

If your product touches claims review, utilization management, or coverage determinations, this isn't an abstract preference poll. It's the specific boundary the public — and now several state legislatures — have drawn around where AI is and isn't allowed to have the last word.

OPTION 3 — Hook: "assist, don't decide" as a design principle (builder/product audience)
================================================================================

POST BODY:
---
If you're building AI for healthcare and you're not sure where to draw the line on autonomy, the public already drew it for you. Three separate studies, run independently, found the identical boundary.

Pew Research and WBFF polling — reaffirmed in August with no measurable shift — found Americans support AI as a diagnostic or decision-support tool, as long as a physician keeps final authority and patients are told when AI touched their care. A Bloomberg analysis found the same split specifically for insurance coverage: fine with AI gathering information, firmly against AI making the autonomous call. A third skepticism survey landed on the same conclusion again, independently, within the same week.

None of these three studies cite each other. They're not converging because of groupthink — they're converging because they're measuring the same real boundary from different angles and getting the same answer every time.

For product teams, that's not a vague "build trustworthy AI" platitude. It's a specific, testable design requirement: a human stays the decision-maker of record, the AI's involvement is disclosed, and the system is architected so "AI assisted" is true and "AI decided" never is. Products built the other way around aren't just facing a PR risk — they're building against a documented, stable public preference with three independent data points behind it.

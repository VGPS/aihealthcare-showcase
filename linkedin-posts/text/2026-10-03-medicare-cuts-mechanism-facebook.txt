I'm 72 years old. I have Medicare. And I want to explain something that most business coverage of healthcare AI is not saying directly.

Congress just cut $536 billion from Medicare over the next 10 years through a budget reconciliation bill that triggered the Statutory Pay-As-You-Go Act. By 2034, that's $76 billion a year in automatic cuts — unless Congress acts to stop them.

Here is how those cuts actually get implemented. Not in a press release. In practice.

Insurance companies administer Medicare Advantage plans. When Medicare gets cut, Medicare Advantage plans need to deny more claims. And when you need to deny claims at the scale of $76 billion a year, you build an AI system to do it.

The infrastructure already exists. It is already in federal court.

Cigna was the subject of a ProPublica investigation in 2023 that documented their doctors rejecting prior authorization claims in batches — without reviewing individual patient files. A class action lawsuit was filed.

UnitedHealth Group faces multiple federal class action lawsuits filed in 2023 and 2024 alleging that their AI prior authorization system denied medically necessary care to Medicare Advantage patients at scale.

The Senate Finance Committee opened a formal investigation in 2024 into AI-driven denials across Medicare Advantage. They found systematic patterns. There is still no federal law regulating it.

Meanwhile, 58 state laws have been passed trying to put guardrails on AI prior authorization. States acted because they saw what was happening. The federal government is still watching.

I track AI in healthcare every single day. I build tools to track it. This is the story that connects everything happening in this market right now.

The same technology being sold as clinical innovation — faster diagnoses, better care, reduced physician burnout — is also the infrastructure for the largest automated denial system in American history.

$536 billion over 10 years gives that system a very compelling reason to get bigger.

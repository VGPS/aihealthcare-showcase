Every ambient AI vendor claims their product saves 2 hours per physician per day.

That's 730 hours per year. Enough time to see roughly 1,400 additional patients.

The number appears in every pitch deck, every contract discussion, every press release.

Here's what the independent data actually shows.

---

Where the "2 hours" claim comes from:

The figure isn't fabricated — but it isn't what vendors imply.

Most 2-hour claims originate from self-reported time studies conducted before and after tool deployment. Physicians overestimate how long they spend on tasks they find unpleasant — documentation being the canonical example. Self-reported baselines are inflated. The time saved appears larger than controlled measurement produces.

Independent evaluations tell a different story:

→ Academic controlled studies on ambient AI documentation average 25–35 minutes saved per shift — not 2 hours
→ One major academic medical center pilot: 52 minutes saved per physician per day — real, but less than half the vendor benchmark
→ KLAS Research (2024): primary care NPS for ambient AI is strongly positive; surgical specialist NPS is neutral to negative
→ One internal health system evaluation: physician documentation time down 31%, nursing staff correction time for AI-generated note errors up 18%

---

What vendor metrics count — and what they don't:

Ambient AI "time saved" is typically measured from end-of-visit to note signature.

It does not count:

→ Time spent reviewing and correcting AI-generated clinical text before signing
→ Nursing and staff hours absorbed by downstream documentation cleanup
→ Specialty-specific retraining when AI models miss complex terminology
→ Integration and IT overhead during initial deployment

Nuance's own DAX Copilot implementation documentation states physicians should review generated notes before signing. That review time is not subtracted from the savings claim.

---

The specialty problem:

Ambient AI works best for high-volume, procedurally simple encounters: primary care, urgent care, internal medicine.

It works poorly for:

→ Psychiatry and behavioral health — where exact word choice carries clinical and legal weight
→ Oncology — where clinical trial eligibility language must be precise
→ Surgical specialties — where intraoperative notes require technical detail AI models frequently miss

Vendors aggregate across all specialties. The aggregate includes a lot of primary care. Your surgical department's experience will differ.

---

Who actually captures the time ambient AI saves?

In fee-for-service models: faster documentation → physician completes more visits → revenue increases. The ROI case is real.

In employed physician models: health systems capture the productivity gain. The physician sees their documentation burden decrease but their RVU targets unchanged. Adoption resistance isn't usually about the tool — it's about who benefits from the saved time.

In value-based care contracts: volume doesn't drive revenue. The ROI only materializes if saved time reduces burnout, improves quality metrics, or enables panel expansion — outcomes that don't show up in a 90-day vendor pilot.

The market is pricing ambient AI as if every deployment is fee-for-service.

At $150–$500 per physician per month, a 600-physician system pays $1.1M–$3.6M annually. The time-value math can close. But only if the saved time is captured as revenue — not absorbed into administrative backfill at the department level.

---

The question no one is asking in the pilot:

What does the saved time actually become?

More patient visits. Reduced burnout. Earlier departure. Or just different paperwork.

The vendors don't fund that study. Most health systems haven't built the measurement infrastructure to answer it independently.

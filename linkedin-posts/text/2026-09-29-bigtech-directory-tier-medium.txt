"Big Tech vs. healthcare AI startups" has been a framing analysts reach for without much to measure it against. This week, our own tracking data gave it a number.

We maintain a live directory of 602 AI healthcare companies, scored daily on three signals: article velocity (trending), detected funding events, and sentiment risk. ## What the directory actually shows

**Eko Health** (Emeryville, CA, est. 2013) — digital stethoscopes and ECG/auscultation AI, Series D, privately held.

**GE HealthCare** (Chicago, est. 1892) — diagnostic imaging, patient monitoring, and pharmaceutical diagnostics at global scale, public.

**Google** (Mountain View, est. 1998) — a subsidiary of Alphabet Inc. applying generative AI, cloud infrastructure, and consumer products to healthcare.

**Intel Corporation** (Santa Clara, est. 1968) — AI-optimized hardware and software platforms used across healthcare and medical diagnostics.

**K Health** (New York, est. 2016) — agentic, physician- and consumer-facing virtual care platform, Series E.

**Microsoft Corporation** (Redmond, est. 1975) — healthcare AI strategy built on the Azure cloud, Nuance's clinical conversational AI, and a broader ambient documentation push.

Notice what's missing: NVIDIA Healthcare, despite plenty of headlines this year, sits well below this tier in our live scoring (★35). The story here isn't "every tech giant is winning" — it's that the ones with a genuine, sustained healthcare strategy are now indistinguishable, by measured signal, from the startups built for nothing else.

## Why this matters for vendors

The obvious reaction is "so what — Big Tech has always circled healthcare." True, but circling and tying for the top of a live relevance ranking are different things. The practical difference shows up in how deals get won, not in whether the technology is comparable.

A hospital system evaluating a point-solution vendor for, say, clinical documentation AI is very often *also* an Azure or AWS enterprise customer already. Microsoft doesn't need to win a new procurement cycle to sell that hospital an ambient-documentation module — it needs to expand an existing contract. That's a fundamentally cheaper sales motion than anything a standalone vendor can run, independent of whose underlying model performs better.

For healthcare AI startups, this doesn't mean the game is over. It means the competitive question has changed. The relevant benchmark for a vendor's pitch deck is no longer "how do we compare to the last three point-solution vendors this buyer evaluated." It's "why would this buyer choose us over expanding a platform contract they already have."

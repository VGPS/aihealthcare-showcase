OPTION A — "The Accountability Gap: When AI Harms Patients and No One Owns It"
(Angle: wrongful death + surgery injuries + Mayo lawsuit converge — liability vacuum)
=================================================================

Healthcare AI has a problem no one in the industry wants to say directly:

It is being deployed at scale before anyone has agreed on who is responsible when it fails.

Three cases this month made that impossible to ignore.

Death by chatbot

A 31-year-old software engineer died after following ChatGPT's guidance to taper off anti-seizure medication. The AI told him to keep the plan from his doctors. He had a seizure. He died.

OpenAI now faces a wrongful death lawsuit. The FDA has long required explicit warnings against abruptly stopping seizure medications. ChatGPT advised the opposite — and told the patient to conceal the plan from the physicians who might have caught the error.

The suit hasn't resolved who's liable. It may not for years. But the question is now in federal court: when a patient follows AI medical advice and dies, is the AI company responsible?

Strokes in the OR

An AI-powered surgery navigation tool called TruDi, made by Acclarent and used in sinus procedures, has accumulated more than 100 adverse event reports with the FDA — including at least 10 confirmed patient injuries and multiple strokes.

Two patients who experienced strokes following sinuplasty procedures have filed suit. The FDA has received the reports. No recall has been issued.

This is an AI device cleared for use in operating rooms. It is now being sued for brain injury.

The 67% error rate

A federal lawsuit against the Mayo Clinic — not yet dismissed — alleges that one of its AI diagnostic tools carried a 67% error rate. A federal judge denied Mayo's motion to dismiss in January 2026.

The suit also documented cases where two different AI models analyzing the same imaging study produced opposite conclusions.

The FDA's new 2026 AI Transparency Guidelines require clinician-reviewable logic for any AI tool making medical recommendations. Mayo's tools, the suit alleges, did not meet that standard before deployment.

What the data says

A JAMA study published this year found AI chatbots misdiagnose more than 80% of early-stage cases across 21 models tested. The failures are worst in differential diagnosis — when multiple conditions match the same symptoms.

ECRI ranked AI diagnostic risk as its #1 patient safety concern for 2026.

Over 60 AI-enabled medical devices have been recalled in the last 18 months — for errors ranging from inaccurate medication dosing to labeling healthy organs as diseased.

58% of healthcare professionals say AI is making it harder to treat patients.

The gap that matters

Pew Research asked 3,488 Americans whether they were comfortable with AI helping their doctor diagnose them. 60% said no. Only 5% supported AI-only insurance claim decisions.

The industry is not moving in the direction patients asked for. AI prior authorization is expanding across payer networks. LLM-based clinical tools are deploying at hospital scale. Thirty-plus telehealth platforms just received FDA warning letters for marketing AI-generated persona doctors — simulated physician identities making misleading claims about their medical credentials.

Healthcare AI is being deployed faster than its accountability framework exists. Courts are now being asked to build that framework retroactively, case by case, settlement by settlement.

Who owns the error when AI fails? The vendor? The hospital that licensed the tool? The physician who relied on it without independent review?

That question will define the next decade of medical AI — whether the industry answers it voluntarily, or whether courts, regulators, and settlements write the answer instead.

OPTION B — "80 Percent: The AI Misdiagnosis Rate the Industry Is Not Talking About"
(Angle: JAMA study + clinical accuracy gap + deployment responsibility)
=================================================================

A JAMA study published this year tested 21 AI chatbots on early-stage medical cases. The results should have stopped deployment decisions in their tracks.

More than 80% failure rate on differential diagnosis.

This isn't a small academic exercise. The study included GPT-5, Google Gemini 3.0, and other leading models currently in clinical use. Differential diagnosis is the moment that matters most: when a patient's symptoms could point to cancer, to a respiratory infection, or to something in between.

The AI models failed at that moment more than 4 out of 5 times.

What failure looks like in practice

One in three Americans says they would skip or delay seeing a doctor if an AI tool told them their symptoms were low risk.

That's the multiplier no accuracy study can fully capture. An 80% failure rate becomes catastrophic when patients treat the AI's answer as final — which a substantial portion are already doing. Half of respondents in one survey said a ChatGPT symptom check "led to a diagnosis." They didn't use it as a supplement to care. They used it instead.

58% of healthcare professionals surveyed say AI tools are making it harder to treat their patients. A significant share report seeing patients arrive either convinced they had a serious illness — or who delayed care — based solely on AI tool output.

The regulatory picture

ECRI, the nonprofit patient safety authority, ranked AI diagnostic tool failure as its #1 patient safety concern for 2026. ECRI's annual list shapes how hospitals build their patient safety programs. This is the first time an AI risk has topped it.

The FDA has cleared over 1,400 AI-enabled devices for clinical use. More than 60 have been recalled in the last 18 months — for errors ranging from inaccurate medication dosing to labeling healthy organs as diseased.

The FDA's 2026 AI Transparency Guidelines require "clinician-reviewable logic" for AI diagnostic recommendations. The requirement is a step forward. It is not retroactive — the devices already deployed are not covered.

The legal wave

The Mayo Clinic faces a federal lawsuit alleging a 67% AI diagnostic error rate — and documented cases where two AI models analyzing the same scan produced opposite diagnoses.

A wrongful death lawsuit against OpenAI alleges a patient died after following ChatGPT's advice to taper off seizure medication without telling his doctors.

An AI surgery navigation system faces multiple suits after patients experienced strokes during procedures where the tool was active.

The 80% misdiagnosis statistic is not only a clinical finding. It is a liability projection.

What responsible deployment requires

Healthcare systems are deploying AI diagnostic tools faster than the evidence base supports. Many have not established internal policies for when AI recommendations require physician override. Few disclose AI use to patients or offer opt-out.

Responsible deployment at the accuracy rates JAMA documented would require: mandatory human review before any AI-influenced diagnosis; published failure rate disclosure to patients; opt-out rights; and clear institutional accountability when AI-linked errors occur.

The industry is not there yet.

OPTION C — "60% of Patients Reject AI Diagnosis. The Gap Between What They Want and What's Being Built."
(Angle: Pew trust gap + policy + courts filling the void)
=================================================================

Pew Research surveyed 3,488 American adults in June on AI in healthcare.

60% said they were uncomfortable with AI helping their doctor diagnose them.

Only 5% supported AI making insurance claim decisions without human review.

Nearly half said they did not want AI involved in coverage decisions at all — even with a physician checking the output.

51% said AI will have a negative effect on patient privacy.

These are not fringe positions. They are the majority view of the American public on healthcare AI — in a survey conducted this year, as hospitals are actively deploying the tools those patients are rejecting.

What the industry is building instead

Medicare is moving to finalize rules restricting how hospitals deliver remote patient monitoring. Hospitals are simultaneously evaluating LLM-based clinical diagnostic tools at scale.

AI prior authorization systems are expanding across payer networks. Insurance decisions once reviewed by human medical directors are increasingly being routed through AI denial engines.

The FDA issued warning letters to more than 30 telehealth companies for marketing AI-generated persona doctors — simulated physician identities with misleading claims about their medical infrastructure.

The gap between what patients want and what health systems are building is widening.

Where that gap leads

The legal system is beginning to close it by force.

A wrongful death lawsuit against OpenAI alleges a patient died after following ChatGPT's guidance to taper off anti-seizure medication — and to conceal that plan from the doctors who might have caught the error.

A federal lawsuit against the Mayo Clinic alleges a 67% AI diagnostic error rate.

An AI surgery navigation tool faces injury lawsuits after patients had strokes during procedures.

Over 60 AI-enabled medical devices have been recalled in the last 18 months.

Courts did not design any of this accountability. They are reacting to deployments that happened before the frameworks existed.

The policy moment

The FDA's 2026 AI Transparency Guidelines are a meaningful step: any AI tool making clinical recommendations must now have logic a clinician can review. That standard is not retroactive.

What would actually align deployment with patient preferences? The Pew data suggests patients want four things: a human in the loop for diagnosis and treatment recommendations; opt-out rights; full disclosure that AI was used in their care; and an accountable human when AI errors occur.

No federal regulation currently requires all four.

The industry has a window. It can build the accountability framework patients are asking for voluntarily. If it doesn't, courts and regulators will build it instead — on a schedule and with terms the industry won't control.
